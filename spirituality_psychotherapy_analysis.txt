PART 1: DOCUMENT CATEGORIZATION
(by application type, method, administration agent, and active Ignatian practice)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CATEGORY 1: IGNATIAN-SPECIFIC THERAPY (ACTIVE IGNATIAN PRACTICE)

Documents:
- Plante, T. G. "What Professional Psychotherapy Practice Can Learn From the
  Jesuits: Introducing Ignatian Spirituality Informed Therapy (I-SIT)."
- Plante, T. G. "St. Ignatius as Psychotherapist? How Jesuit Spirituality and
  Wisdom Can Enhance Psychotherapy."

Application type: Direct clinical integration of a named spiritual tradition
into a treatment model (I-SIT).
Method: Adaptation of the Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola
(c. 1522-1524) into therapeutic technique.
Administration agent: Licensed psychotherapist (not clergy); the spiritual
director's role is translated into the clinician's role.

Active Ignatian practices, tied to Ignatius's teachings:
- Examen (daily review of consciousness) -> structured self-reflection /
  journaling as session homework
- Discernment of spirits -> evaluating thoughts/impulses for movement toward
  or away from wellbeing (consolation vs. desolation as affect tracking)
- Indifference/detachment (Principle and Foundation) -> acceptance-based
  coping, parallel to Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)
- Imaginative contemplation -> guided imagery technique
- Magis ("the more") -> values-driven goal setting
- Cura personalis (care of the whole person) -> whole-person case
  conceptualization

CATEGORY 2: TRADITION-SPECIFIC, BROADER CATHOLIC FRAMEWORK

Document:
- Plante, T. G. (2023). "What Is Catholic Psychotherapy and How Should It
  Move Forward."

Application type: Denominationally-framed therapy; Ignatian content present
but subsumed under Catholic anthropology generally.
Method: Integration of Catholic moral/theological commitments into case
formulation.
Administration agent: Clinician, typically self-identified Catholic or
working with Catholic clients.

CATEGORY 3: GENERAL SPIRITUALITY-PSYCHOTHERAPY INTEGRATION
(NON-TRADITION-SPECIFIC)

Documents:
- Plante, T. G. (2006). "The Very Best Science and Practice Must Come
  Together When It Comes to Spirituality and Psychotherapy Integration."
- Plante, T. G. (2016). "Principles of Incorporating Spirituality Into
  Professional Clinical Practice."
- Pargament, K. (2007). "Introduction to the Special Issue on Spirituality
  and Psychotherapy."
- Worthington, E. (2009). "Next Steps for Clinicians in Religious and
  Spiritual Therapy: An Endpiece."
- Cullen, A. "The Human Experience of Spirituality: A New Perspective for
  Therapy."

Application type: Framework/guideline literature; spirituality as a generic
clinical variable, not a practiced discipline.
Method: Evidence-based integration principles, assessment of client
religiosity/spirituality, ethical guidelines.
Administration agent: Secular or religiously-unaffiliated clinician applying
spirituality-informed competencies.
Ignatian practice: None active. Plante's general-integration papers carry
Ignatian influence in ethos (discernment-like decision frameworks) but
prescribe no exercises.

CATEGORY 4: PSYCHIATRIC / DIAGNOSTIC PERSPECTIVE

Document:
- Verhagen, P. J. (2011). "Spirituality, Religion and Psychopathology:
  Towards an Integrative Psychiatry."

Application type: Psychiatric nosology and integration at the medical level.
Method: Differentiating spiritual experience from psychopathology;
integrative psychiatric assessment.
Administration agent: Psychiatrist / medical clinician.
Ignatian practice: None.

CATEGORY 5: PRACTITIONER EXPERIENCE / EMPIRICAL-QUALITATIVE

Documents:
- Cunha, V. "Religiosity/Spirituality (R/S) in the Clinical Context:
  Professional Experiences of Psychotherapists."
- Coyle, A. (2011). "Religion, Spirituality and Therapeutic Practice."

Application type: Descriptive/empirical study of how clinicians handle R/S;
not a treatment model.
Method: Qualitative interviews; survey of practitioner attitudes/behaviors.
Administration agent: Practicing psychotherapists as research subjects
rather than agents delivering a spiritual method.
Ignatian practice: None.

CORPUS NOTE: Only the two Plante I-SIT papers contain operationalized
Ignatian technique. The remaining nine documents range from denominational
framing to entirely secular meta-discussion. Any synthesis treating the set
as uniformly "Ignatian-informed" would misrepresent nine of eleven documents.

PART 2: CORE PHILOSOPHIES (SUMMARY, NO REFERENCES)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE IGNATIAN/JESUIT STRAND rests on the premise that a 500-year-old
contemplative system anticipated modern psychotherapy. Central commitments:
regular structured self-examination of one's inner life; "discernment" —
disciplined evaluation of inner movements (impulses, desires, moods) to
determine which lead toward flourishing and which toward destruction;
cultivated detachment from outcomes one cannot control; use of imagination
as a vehicle for insight; orientation of life around deeply held values
rather than comfort; and care for the whole person — body, mind, spirit —
rather than symptom reduction alone. The philosophical claim is that these
map almost directly onto cognitive monitoring, affect labeling,
acceptance-based therapy, guided imagery, and values work, meaning the
tradition can be secularized into technique without requiring the client's
religious belief.

THE CATHOLIC-FRAMEWORK STRAND goes further, holding that therapy is not
value-neutral and that a coherent anthropology — a defined view of human
nature, purpose, and morality — should ground treatment rather than being
bracketed out. Its core tension: fidelity to doctrine versus empirical
accountability.

THE GENERAL INTEGRATION STRAND takes the opposite position: spirituality
matters clinically not because any tradition is true, but because it is a
dimension of clients' lived experience that affects outcomes. Commitments
are pragmatic and ethical — assess religiosity like any other client
variable, respect autonomy, never impose belief, and subject all spiritual
interventions to the same evidence standards as any other technique.
Spirituality is treated as a resource or a stressor, never as a framework
the clinician endorses.

THE PSYCHIATRIC STRAND is concerned with boundary-drawing: distinguishing
genuine spiritual experience from pathology, and resisting both the
medicalization of religion and the romanticization of symptoms as mystical.

THE EMPIRICAL-PRACTITIONER STRAND holds that what matters is what
clinicians actually do — and finds they are largely untrained, ambivalent,
and improvising when religion enters the room, which undercuts the tidy
frameworks the other strands propose.

UNRESOLVED FAULT LINE: whether spirituality in therapy is a TOOL to be
extracted and tested, or a WORLDVIEW that cannot be instrumentalized
without losing what made it effective.

PART 3: THE STRIPPED-DOWN MECHANICS
(what actually happens between clinician and client, religion removed)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

One person (the clinician) holds structural authority, a procedural script,
and an interpretive framework. The other (the client) brings raw,
disorganized internal experience. The enterprise consists of the first
person directing the second person's attention according to a schedule and
a method.

THE OPERATIONS:

1. ATTENTION TRAINING. The clinician instructs the client to repeatedly
   observe their own mental content at set intervals — what they felt,
   when, in response to what. Converts vague distress into discrete,
   nameable events. The client is taught to generate data about themselves.
   [Cf. self-monitoring in CBT: Beck, A. T. (1979). Cognitive Therapy and
   the Emotional Disorders; cf. the Ignatian Examen.]

2. SORTING. The client is taught a classification scheme: these internal
   states move you toward what you say you want; these move you away. The
   clinician supplies the taxonomy; the client applies it until the
   evaluative grid is internalized.
   [Cf. Ignatian discernment of spirits; cf. cognitive restructuring.]

3. DECOUPLING. The client is trained to register a desire or fear without
   acting on it or being commanded by it — manipulation of the relationship
   between stimulus and response.
   [Cf. Ignatian indifference; cf. cognitive defusion in ACT: Hayes, S. C.,
   Strosahl, K., & Wilson, K. G. (1999). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy.]

4. SIMULATION. The clinician has the client run vivid imagined scenarios to
   produce emotional responses in a controlled setting where they can be
   examined rather than merely suffered.
   [Cf. Ignatian imaginative contemplation; cf. imaginal exposure: Foa, E.
   B., & Kozak, M. J. (1986). "Emotional processing of fear." Psychological
   Bulletin, 99(1), 20-35.]

5. VALUE ANCHORING. The clinician elicits statements about what the client
   claims matters most, then repeatedly holds the client's actual behavior
   against those statements. Discrepancy generates discomfort; discomfort
   drives behavioral change.
   [Cf. the Magis; cf. values clarification in ACT; cf. cognitive
   dissonance: Festinger, L. (1957). A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.]

6. THE RELATIONAL SUBSTRATE. All of the above runs on a sanctioned,
   asymmetric relationship in which one person discloses everything and the
   other discloses almost nothing, where the discloser's statements are
   received without social penalty. The client's nervous system recalibrates
   around an interaction that doesn't punish honesty.
   [Cf. the therapeutic alliance literature: Bordin, E. S. (1979). "The
   generalizability of the psychoanalytic concept of the working alliance."
   Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice, 16(3), 252-260; Rogers, C.
   (1957). "The necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic
   personality change." Journal of Consulting Psychology, 21(2), 95-103.]

SUMMARY: scheduled self-observation, externally supplied sorting criteria,
response inhibition, controlled imaginative exposure, and value-behavior
confrontation — administered through an asymmetric authority relationship
the subject consents to and pays for. Every tradition examined, religious
or secular, is a different branding and justification narrative wrapped
around these same operations. Religious versions claim an external referent
for the sorting criteria; secular versions claim an empirical one. In the
room, the operations are identical.

PART 4: 3D COORDINATE MAPPING OF EMOTIONAL/MENTAL STATES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FORMAL PRECEDENT: The PAD (Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance) dimensional model.
[Mehrabian, A., & Russell, J. A. (1974). An Approach to Environmental
Psychology. MIT Press; Russell, J. A. (1980). "A circumplex model of
affect." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 39(6), 1161-1178;
Russell, J. A., & Mehrabian, A. (1977). "Evidence for a three-factor theory
of emotions." Journal of Research in Personality, 11(3), 273-294.]

THE THREE AXES (each scaled -10 to +10):

X — VALENCE: hedonic tone. +X = pleasure/contentment; -X = distress/misery.
    (The happy/sad axis.)
Y — AROUSAL: nervous-system activation. +Y = agitated, energized, vigilant;
    -Y = flat, lethargic, shut down.
Z — DOMINANCE (AGENCY): perceived control. +Z = "I act on the world";
    -Z = "the world acts on me."

Every emotional state is a point (x, y, z).

DIAGNOSTIC STATES AS COORDINATES (illustrative):
- Panic:                  (-7, +9, -8)  miserable, maximally activated,
                                        zero control
- Depression:             (-8, -7, -6)  miserable, deactivated, helpless
- Rage:                   (-6, +8, +5)  negative valence but HIGH dominance
                                        (why anger feels better than fear)
- Anxiety:                (-5, +6, -5)
- Numbness/dissociation:  ( 0, -9, -4)  the dangerous one — valence reads
                                        neutral while the system is offline
- Mania:                  (+8, +10, +9) maximum-positive on all axes is
                                        NOT health

THE TARGET LOCATION:
Not (+10, y, z). Approximately (+3 to +5, -2 to +2, +4 to +6): mildly
positive, mid-band arousal, solidly but not grandiosely agentic. Clinical
work aims at a REGION, not a corner — and at MOBILITY: capacity to move
through the space appropriately and return. A person locked at (+4, 0, +5)
who cannot reach grief when grief is warranted is also pathological.
Health = a home region plus freedom of travel.
[Cf. emotional flexibility/psychological flexibility: Kashdan, T. B., &
Rottenberg, J. (2010). "Psychological flexibility as a fundamental aspect
of health." Clinical Psychology Review, 30(7), 865-878.]

THE OPERATIONS MAPPED ONTO THE GEOMETRY:
- Attention training = teaching the subject to read their own current
  coordinates. Most clients arrive unable to locate themselves; they report
  "bad" for every point in negative-X space.
  [Cf. alexithymia and affect-labeling research: Lieberman, M. D., et al.
  (2007). "Putting feelings into words." Psychological Science, 18(5),
  421-428.]
- Sorting = labeling VECTORS, not points. The taxonomy classifies
  movements: this thought pulls toward (-8, +7, -6); this behavior moves
  toward the target region.
- Decoupling = breaking the automatic coupling between a stimulus and a
  fixed displacement. The trigger still fires; the magnitude of the induced
  vector shrinks.
- Simulation = deliberately transporting the subject to a hostile
  coordinate inside a container where Z is artificially supported, so the
  location loses its terror through visitation.
- Value anchoring = installing the destination coordinates. Values define
  where on the grid the person's life is supposed to be lived; discrepancy
  between current and anchored position generates the motive force.
- The asymmetric relationship = the clinician as fixed reference point
  (origin marker) against which the subject calibrates. The client's
  instrumentation is unreliable; the clinician's steadier read provides
  triangulation until the client's own positioning system is trained.

LIMITS OF THE MODEL:
1. The axes are not orthogonal in real organisms (arousal and valence
   covary).
2. The space is not Euclidean — the distance from numbness to grief is
   shorter than it looks, and grief can be progress.
3. People are not single points; they occupy distributions, sometimes
   bimodal ones.
The grid is a navigation aid, not the territory. Treating it as literal —
optimizing a human toward a coordinate — reproduces the instrumentalization
problem flagged in Part 2's fault line.
